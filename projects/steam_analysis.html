Steam Game Sales & Trends Analysis

Exploring how price, reviews, genre, and engagement relate to a game's commercial success — using 34,154 Steam titles released from 2023 onward.

🗄 SQLite 📊 Tableau Public 🔟 10 correlations examined
Executive Summary

All relationships described below are correlational, not causal — the data shows what tends to appear together, not what directly causes what.

The central finding is that no single factor reliably predicts a game's commercial success. Price alone doesn't predict ownership — the highest-selling games in this dataset appear at multiple price points ($22, $40, $60), not just the cheapest end. Review scores alone don't predict ownership either — plenty of well-reviewed games have small audiences. But the two do interact in an important way: virtually no game with very high ownership has poor reviews, suggesting strong reviews function as a floor that enables massive success, without being a guarantee of it on their own.

The single cleanest relationship in the dataset is between average playtime and review sentiment: games that hold players' attention for thousands of hours are, almost without exception, rated 85% or higher positive. At the genre level, Action, Indie, and Adventure dominate total ownership by volume, while Sports, RPG, and Racing command the highest average prices — Indie in particular achieves outsized total reach despite a modest average price, reflecting a high-volume, lower-price strategy that outperforms higher-priced genres in aggregate.

Beyond the findings themselves, this project surfaced a genuine data quality issue worth highlighting: a pre-calculated review-percentage field in the source dataset was silently incorrect for roughly half of all games. This was caught by spot-checking individual titles against their raw review counts, not by trusting the field at face value — a reminder that ready-made columns in any dataset deserve verification before being relied upon.

The process

A quick walkthrough of how the raw dataset became the analysis above — including the detours.

1

Import & initial cleanup

The raw Kaggle dataset had 89,618 rows and 47 columns, many with no analytical value (image URLs, support links, long descriptions). Reduced to 24 relevant columns using SQL.

2

Filtering to 2023+

Filtered to games released 2023 onward to keep the analysis current, reducing the dataset to 34,590 rows.

3

Fixing the ownership field

Steam's estimated_owners field came as a text range (e.g. "50000000 - 100000000"). Parsed into a clean numeric midpoint for actual analysis.

4

Splitting multi-value fields

Genres, categories, and tags were stored as Python-style list strings in a single cell. Split into proper junction tables using recursive SQL — 101K+ genre pairs, 131K+ category pairs, 392K+ tag pairs.

🔀 Detour: the plan was to split these inside Tableau, but this version of Tableau Public didn't expose a Pivot option for text-file sources. Moved the splitting back into SQL instead of forcing a workaround.
5

Separating games from non-game software

Genre exploration revealed 436 entries were actually software tools (Accounting, Video Production, etc.), not games. Split into a separate table to keep the main analysis accurate.

6

Removing unrealistic price outliers

Three entries priced at $200, $500, and $1000 were distorting the price-vs-ownership chart. Excluded prices above $100 as a documented judgment call, not a definitive rule.

7

Discovering and correcting a source data bug

An unexpectedly low genre-level review average led to spot-checking individual titles. Found that the source dataset's pct_pos_total field silently defaulted to -1 for ~17,000 games — nearly half the dataset — even when real review data existed underneath.

🐛 Verified against raw positive/negative counts, then built a corrected field (pct_positive_calc) computed directly from the reliable underlying numbers — average 80.1% across reviewed games.
8

Connecting tables in Tableau

Tableau Public has no native SQLite connector, so each table was exported as CSV and connected via relationships on appid in a hub-and-spoke structure.

Key findings

Ten correlations examined across pricing, reviews, genre, and engagement.

Finding 1
Average Price by Genre
Sports and RPG carry the highest average prices (~$8.50), followed by Racing (~$7) — consistent with longer, content-heavy, or licensed franchise experiences. Free to Play sits at essentially $0 as expected.
📌 Data note: Free to Play averages a small non-zero price (~$0.07), traced to 49 games carrying the tag alongside a real price — likely describing an associated feature like a free trial mode, not a labeling error.
Finding 2
Metacritic Score vs. Estimated Owners
A large share of games — including some with 75M+ estimated owners — have no Metacritic score at all. Among scored games, only a weak upward trend exists between score and ownership. Metacritic coverage skews toward higher-profile titles.
Finding 3
Player Review % vs. Estimated Owners
Using the corrected review field, the overall relationship stays weak — but every single high-ownership outlier (35M-75M+ owners) sits in the 50-97% positive range. Positive reviews may be a necessary but not sufficient condition for massive success.
Finding 4
Price vs. Estimated Owners
After removing price outliers, the highest-ownership hits (~75M owners) appear at three distinct price points — $22, $40, and $60 — not clustered at the cheapest end. Price alone isn't a strong predictor of reaching a massive audience.
Finding 5
Average Playtime vs. Review %
The cleanest relationship in the dataset. Games with 8,000-20,000+ average playtime hours sit almost universally in the 85-97% positive range — no exceptions among the highest-playtime titles.
Finding 6
Achievements vs. Average Playtime
Counterintuitively, games with the most achievements (1,000+) show near-zero average playtime, while the highest-playtime games have relatively few achievements. Achievement count doesn't appear to drive long-term engagement.
Finding 7
Total Estimated Ownership by Genre
Action dominates total ownership (~800M), with Indie and Adventure close behind (~700M each). Indie punches far above its price-point weight — high volume at a lower price outperforms pricier genres in aggregate.
Finding 8
Average Review % by Genre
Most genres cluster in a realistic 75-85% positive range. Massively Multiplayer is the clear exception at ~65%, possibly reflecting live-service pain points like server stability or monetization complaints.
Finding 9
Discount vs. Estimated Owners
The biggest commercial hits sit almost entirely at 0% discount. Discounting looks more like a tool for underperforming titles than something that drives games to massive scale.
Finding 10
Peak Concurrent Users vs. Estimated Owners
Mostly tracks with ownership as expected, with one notable exception: a title with ~700K peak concurrent users but comparatively modest ownership — an unusually concentrated, highly active community.

This project intentionally documents the detours, not just the final result — the Tableau pivot limitation, the non-game software mixed into the data, and especially the source dataset's review-percentage bug are all examples of the normal, unglamorous problem-solving that real data analysis involves. Catching and correcting the review bug in particular came from a habit worth having with any dataset: spot-check pre-calculated fields against their raw values before trusting them, especially when a result looks surprising.
