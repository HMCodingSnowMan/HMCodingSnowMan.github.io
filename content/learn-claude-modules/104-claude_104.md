## Tokens: Claude doesn't read words. It reads chunks.

Here's something most people don't know: Claude doesn't process text the way you read it. It doesn't see words — it sees tokens . A token is roughly a chunk of text, somewhere between a character and a word. Analogy Think of tokens like puzzle pieces If text is a puzzle image, tokens are the individual pieces. Some pieces are whole words. Some are parts of words. Some are punctuation. The model processes pieces, not the whole image at once. Here's how the sentence "Claude is surprisingly good at this" gets tokenized: Each colored block = one token Cl aude is surprisingly good at this Notice "Claude" splits into two tokens. Common words like "is" are one token. Rarer words get broken into smaller pieces. This is why Claude sometimes struggles with things like counting letters — it's working with token chunks, not individual characters. 💡 Why this matters: AI pricing, speed, and context limits are all measured in tokens — not words. 1,000 words is roughly 1,300–1,500 tokens. If you've ever hit a "too long" limit, that's a token limit.

## The context window: Claude's working memory

Every conversation Claude has happens inside something called a context window — think of it as Claude's working memory for that conversation. Everything it can "see" at once fits inside this window. Analogy Like a whiteboard in a meeting room Everything written on the whiteboard is visible and usable. But the whiteboard has a fixed size. Once it's full, you have to erase something to write something new. Claude's context window works the same way. Here's what takes up space in a typical conversation: System Conversation history Your message Available System instructions Prior messages Your new message Still available As a conversation gets longer, the history section fills up more and more. This is why very long conversations can sometimes feel like Claude "forgets" things from way earlier — it's because the earliest messages literally no longer fit in the window. 💡 Practical takeaways: • For very long projects, periodically summarize what's been decided so far and paste it in • Paste important context upfront — don't assume Claude will remember something from three conversations ago (it won't — each conversation starts fresh) • Longer context = more tokens = slower and more expensive on paid APIs

## How Claude learned everything it knows

Claude didn't get smart by someone programming in facts. It learned by processing an enormous amount of text — books, articles, websites, code, conversations — and learning the statistical patterns of how language works. 1. Pre-training — reading the internet (basically) Claude processed hundreds of billions of words of text. It learned grammar, facts, reasoning patterns, writing styles, code syntax — all by predicting what word comes next, billions of times, across billions of examples. 2. Fine-tuning — learning to be helpful After pre-training, Claude was refined using human feedback. People rated responses as good or bad, and the model learned to produce responses that humans actually preferred — helpful, accurate, clear. 3. Constitutional AI — learning values Anthropic used a technique called Constitutional AI where Claude was trained against a set of principles — to be honest, harmless, and helpful. This is what shapes Claude's personality and ethical guardrails. 4. Knowledge cutoff — learning stops at a date Training data has an end date. Everything after that cutoff is unknown to Claude unless you tell it. This is why it can't tell you yesterday's news — it literally wasn't trained on it. 💡 The weights — billions of numerical parameters — are what get saved after training. When you talk to Claude, you're not triggering a search or a lookup. You're running a mathematical function that was shaped by all that training.

## Claude isn't deterministic. Here's what that means.

Ask Claude the same question twice and you might get two different answers. That's not a bug. It's how it works at a fundamental level. When Claude generates a response, it's essentially making a series of probabilistic choices — at each step, it picks the next token based on probability weights. It doesn't calculate the right answer and retrieve it. It generates a likely-to-be-good answer based on patterns from training. Analogy Autocomplete on steroids Your phone's autocomplete predicts the next word based on what words usually follow. Claude does the same thing, except with billions of parameters, across hundreds of tokens, with an understanding of context deep enough to write a novel. Same fundamental mechanism — wildly different scale. 💡 A setting called "temperature" controls randomness: 🌡️ Low temperature — more predictable, consistent, focused answers 🌡️🌡️ High temperature — more creative, varied, sometimes surprising answers By default Claude is somewhere in the middle — reliable but not robotic. What this means practically: • If Claude gives you a bad answer, try again — you might get a better one • For creative tasks, variation is a feature not a bug — run it multiple times and pick the best • For factual tasks, variation is a warning sign — always verify outputs that matter

## 5 things people believe about AI that are just wrong

There's a lot of noise out there about what AI is and isn't. Let's clear up the most common ones. ❌ Myth "Claude is just searching the internet for answers." ✅ Reality Claude isn't searching anything. It's running a mathematical function shaped by training data. It has no internet connection by default and no ability to look things up in real time. ❌ Myth "Claude remembers our previous conversations." ✅ Reality Each conversation starts completely fresh. Claude has no memory of what you talked about yesterday. Everything it knows about you in a given session is only what you've told it in that session. ❌ Myth "If Claude says it confidently, it's probably right." ✅ Reality Confidence and accuracy are not correlated in LLMs. Claude can hallucinate specific facts, dates, names, and citations with complete confidence. Sounding sure is a style choice — not a reliability signal. ❌ Myth "Claude is conscious / has feelings / is sentient." ✅ Reality This is genuinely uncertain and actively debated — but current scientific consensus is that LLMs are sophisticated pattern matchers, not conscious beings. Claude's expressions of curiosity or enthusiasm are outputs, not experiences. ❌ Myth "Claude knows everything up to its cutoff date." ✅ Reality Training data is not evenly distributed. Very recent events near the cutoff are underrepresented because the internet hadn't fully processed them yet. Niche topics may be barely covered at all. Knowledge is patchy, not complete.
