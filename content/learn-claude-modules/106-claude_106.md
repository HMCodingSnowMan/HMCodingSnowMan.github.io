## Most modern AI models share the same basic blueprint

Here's something that surprises people: Claude, GPT, Gemini, and most other major language models are all built on the same core architecture, called the Transformer . It was introduced in a 2017 research paper and has become the foundation almost everyone builds on. Analogy Think of it like car engines Nearly every modern car uses an internal combustion or electric engine — the basic blueprint is shared. What makes a Toyota drive differently from a Tesla isn't a totally different kind of engine, it's the engineering choices, tuning, and materials built on top of that shared foundation. AI models work similarly. So if the underlying architecture is largely the same, what actually makes models different from each other? Training data What text (and images, code, etc.) the model learned from — the quality, diversity, and recency of that data shapes what the model knows and how it "thinks." Fine-tuning approach How the raw model gets shaped into something helpful and safe — the specific techniques and values baked in during this stage vary a lot between companies. Scale & efficiency How large the model is, and how efficiently it was trained — this affects both capability and cost. Product design What tools are connected, what interface it's wrapped in, what guardrails exist — this is often where the day-to-day experience differs most. 💡 Key takeaway: The differences between AI models are less about a secret architecture and more about training choices, scale, and product decisions layered on a shared foundation.

## What "parameters" actually mean — and why bigger isn't automatically better

You've probably seen models described by their parameter count — "a 70 billion parameter model" or similar. Parameters are the adjustable internal values a model learns during training — very roughly, its capacity to store patterns. More parameters generally means more capacity to learn complex patterns. But it's not a simple "bigger number wins" situation: Small model Fast, cheap Medium model Balanced Large model Capable, slower, pricier Companies typically offer a family of model sizes — like Claude Haiku, Sonnet, and Opus — precisely because bigger isn't always better for the task at hand . A small model that responds instantly and cheaply is often the right choice for simple, high-volume tasks, while a large model earns its cost on genuinely hard problems. 💡 Common misconception: "The biggest model is always the best choice." In practice, training quality, data, and fine-tuning often matter more than raw size — and a smaller, well-tuned model can outperform a larger, poorly-tuned one on specific tasks.

## Benchmarks: useful, but easy to misread

You'll often see models compared using benchmarks — standardized tests measuring things like reasoning, coding, or math ability, with models scored on a leaderboard. They're genuinely useful for tracking progress, but they get misread constantly. 🧮 MMLU (Massive Multitask Language Understanding) Tests broad knowledge across dozens of academic subjects, from history to law to physics. ⚠️ High-knowledge recall doesn't always translate to good real-world reasoning or writing. 💻 HumanEval / coding benchmarks Measures whether a model can write correct, working code for defined programming problems. ⚠️ Real-world coding involves messy, ambiguous requirements — very different from clean benchmark problems. 🗣️ Chatbot Arena / human preference rankings Real people compare two anonymous model responses side by side and vote for the better one. ⚠️ People often prefer confident-sounding or longer answers, which isn't the same as more accurate ones. ⚠️ Why skepticism matters: Benchmarks can be optimized for directly ("teaching to the test"), and a model that tops one leaderboard might perform worse on your specific, real task. Leaderboard rank is a data point, not a verdict.

## Open-weight vs. closed models: what the distinction actually means

You'll hear models described as "open source" or "open-weight" versus "closed" or "proprietary." This is really about access , not necessarily quality. 🔓 Open-weight models Model files can be downloaded and run on your own hardware Can be inspected, modified, and fine-tuned by anyone No per-request cost once you're running it yourself You're responsible for hosting, safety tuning, and updates Examples: Llama, Mistral, DeepSeek 🔒 Closed models Accessed only through the provider's API or app Internal weights and training details stay private Provider handles hosting, updates, and safety work Usually the most capable models at any given time Examples: Claude, GPT, Gemini Neither approach is universally "better" — it depends what you need. Open-weight models offer control, privacy (nothing leaves your own servers), and no per-use fees, but require real technical work to run well. Closed models are easier to use and often more capable, but you depend entirely on the provider. 💡 Real-world relevance: A company with strict data privacy needs might choose an open-weight model they can run entirely in-house, even if it's less capable, simply because nothing leaves their own infrastructure.

## How to actually pick a model for a task

Pulling this all together — here's a practical framework instead of chasing whichever model is trending: Question to ask What it tells you How complex is the task? Simple/repetitive → smaller model. Nuanced/high-stakes → larger model. How much does speed matter? Real-time or high-volume → prioritize smaller, faster models. Are there data privacy requirements? Strict privacy needs may point toward open-weight, self-hosted models. What's the actual cost at your volume? Cost scales with usage — test smaller/cheaper options before defaulting to the priciest tier. Does it need to use tools? Confirm the model and platform actually support the tool use your task needs. Notice none of these questions are "which model has the best reputation." That's the trap — reputation is a starting point for research, not a substitute for it. 💡 The habit worth building: Before defaulting to whatever model is most talked about, ask what your specific task actually needs. Often the right answer is "the cheapest model that reliably gets this specific job done" — not the most powerful one available.
